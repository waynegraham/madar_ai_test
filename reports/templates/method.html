A guide for readers

How this experiment works

We ask whether computer-generated suggestions can help scholars find and examine features of manuscripts. The manuscript remains the evidence; a machine’s account of it remains a proposal until it has been reviewed.

The processKey termsWhat the results can tell us

From manuscript to possible discovery

This is the intended sequence of the experiment. It describes a method, not a claim that every manuscript has completed every step.

  1. Original manuscript

    We begin with a digital image of the manuscript page and retain the original file unchanged. Smaller display copies help us examine the page; detailed crops come from the original image.

  2. Segmentation

    The system proposes boundaries around parts of the page, such as a passage of text or a mark in the margin.

  3. Region classification

    Each region receives a proposed description, such as main text, handwritten annotation, or stamp or seal. A boundary and a description are separate suggestions: either can need correction.

  4. Script and language identification

    The system suggests which writing system and language may be present. These are different questions: identifying Arabic script does not by itself establish that the language is Arabic.

  5. HTR

    The system attempts to transcribe the visible writing. The aim is to retain original spelling and line breaks, without translation or silent correction; whether it succeeds must be checked against the image.

  6. Human correction in eScriptorium

    A scholar examines the page and corrects region boundaries, labels, and transcriptions as needed. Human-reviewed eScriptorium annotations are the authoritative reference for evaluation within the scope of that review; uncorrected machine output from eScriptorium is still a prediction.

  7. Evaluation

    We compare predictions with the human-reviewed reference. Without that reference, we can describe differences between systems, but cannot call their agreement accuracy.

  8. Potential corpus-scale discovery

    Reviewed features and readings could eventually help scholars find comparable material across many manuscripts. Any proposed connection would still need examination in its historical context.

How the current experiments fit. The models propose region boundaries and types together. Script, language, and transcription are also requested together, while the saved HTR experiments use documented shared input regions, which may come from human-drawn boxes, machine ALTO layout, or a whole image. Human correction and evaluation are therefore not completed merely because a machine result appears in the report; each manuscript page identifies the reference material actually available.

Key terms

Segmentation
Dividing a page image into regions that can be examined separately. A proposed region might enclose a block of writing, a marginal note, or a seal.
HTR
Handwritten text recognition: using a computer to turn an image of handwriting into text. The result is a suggested transcription, not a verified reading.
Vision-language model
A computer model trained to work with images and language. It can respond to a manuscript image with descriptions or proposed transcriptions, but a fluent response does not establish historical understanding.
Ground truth
The human-reviewed reference used to assess a system’s results. In this project, human-corrected eScriptorium annotations can serve as that reference; machine-generated ALTO files cannot become ground truth simply by being exported from eScriptorium.
CER
Character error rate measures how many character insertions, deletions, or substitutions are needed to turn a proposed transcription into the reference text, divided by the reference’s character count. It measures textual differences, not the scholarly importance of a misreading.
WER
Word error rate makes the same kind of comparison using words rather than characters. A low rate does not guarantee that a crucial name, date, or phrase has been read correctly.
Visual similarity
A resemblance in appearance, such as similar shapes, layouts, or marks. Resemblance alone does not establish that two marks have the same owner, institution, date, or meaning.

CER and WER are shown only when a linked human-corrected transcription is available. This report does not silently normalize spelling or Unicode text; its technical details explain how characters, words, and line breaks are compared.

Observation and interpretation

What the results can tell us

Machine results are suggestions. Several systems can agree and still be mistaken. A high confidence value is a model’s estimate, not scholarly certainty or a substitute for checking the manuscript.

Detecting something and interpreting it are different tasks. A system may locate a seal without reading its inscription or establishing whose seal it was. A region label records a proposed visible feature; historical significance requires further evidence.

Waqf belongs initially to semantic interpretation. Here, that means interpreting meaning and function, rather than treating “waqf” as another visual region type. Relevant evidence might occur in a passage, an annotation, a seal, or several parts of a document considered together.

Human review establishes the reference. Its scope matters: corrected boundaries do not automatically imply corrected text. Review decisions and unresolved readings should remain visible and open to scholarly revision.
